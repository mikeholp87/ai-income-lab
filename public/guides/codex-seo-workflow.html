Quick answer

Give Codex the research you want to apply, ask it to audit your site against that research, and have it write the findings to a report. Turn the report into a phased implementation plan saved in your repo, run the plan with a capable model, then ask Codex to run the same audit again. Review the diff before you deploy. The same loop works for most on-page SEO jobs. In this guide I use internal linking as the example.

Codex is good at SEO tasks that touch many files at once, such as adding links across hundreds of articles. This guide follows what I did on my own blog. For general Codex habits, see our Codex workflow guide.

1. Why internal linking suits Codex

Internal linking is tedious by hand. You need to know every article, find related pages, and place links in sensible spots. On a large site, the effort depends on the content structure and the checks already available.

Codex can read the whole content folder, count links per page, find pages with no inbound links (orphans), and edit files in bulk. You keep control by reviewing the plan and the diff.

2. Pick your research source

Choose the research source with care. In my video, I walk through a Black Hat World forum post where one SEO describes testing internal links across his own sites. The claims I quoted from it include:

  • Pages with 10 or more internal links did better for him, and he saw no ranking drop with 20 or more
  • Links higher on the page seemed to carry more weight
  • Links that people actually click seemed to matter

That is one person's experience, not a controlled study. When I asked Codex to plan from it, Codex itself called the core rule "useful but anecdotal." Use it as a working hypothesis and measure your own results.

You could also feed Codex Google's own documentation or your keyword data. Our DataForSEO MCP guide shows one way to pull that data.

3. Set up the repo

Before you start:

  • Commit or stash your work so the changes are easy to review.
  • Define the project instructions and acceptance checks before a bulk edit. In my video Codex used an internal linking optimizer skill I had installed from skills.sh. You don't need it: plain prompts plus your own acceptance checks do the same job.

4. Run the first audit

My prompt was simple. I pasted the research and asked:

Create an internal linking plan based on all of the research in this article.

Codex mapped the principles onto my blog's structure, then ran the repo's existing audit tooling to get real counts. The results on my site were much worse than I expected. Most articles were far below 10 internal body links, and hundreds had zero inbound links from other posts.

It wrote the findings to a report file. Read it in full. This is where you catch rules that do not fit your site.

5. Write an implementation plan

Next, ask Codex to turn the report into a plan saved in the repo, for example under docs/plans/. Mine split the work into phases:

  1. Repair high-value pages first by linking from the strongest articles to priority orphans
  2. Bring every article up to the body link minimum
  3. Complete orphan and cluster coverage, using hub and spoke clusters
  4. Navigation, click tracking and maintenance

The plan also set a mix for each article. Mine called for at least six links to closely related posts, at least two to guides or tools, and at least two to product pages, with the first contextual link early in the article where it reads naturally.

Edit the plan if you disagree with any of it. It is much cheaper to change a plan than to undo hundreds of edits.

6. Implement in phases

For planning I used a lighter model. For the edits I switched to a stronger one. Then:

Implement the plan in docs/plans/internal-linking.md, phase by phase.

7. Re-audit and fix the stragglers

After implementation, ask Codex to run the same audit again and confirm the rules are met across the whole blog. Mine took about a minute and a half. It reported zero orphans and every public English article inside the target link range, with a small set of pages still below the inbound link target.

Then a short follow-up:

Fix the remaining under-linked pages.

Repeat until your agreed checks pass. Do not force irrelevant links just to hit the forum post’s anecdotal numeric target.

8. Review before you deploy

Bulk link edits can go wrong in quiet ways: links to the wrong page, repeated anchors, or links stuffed into intros. Before you deploy:

  • Ask for a read-only review against the base branch, using the supported review controls in your installed client.
  • Spot-check ten random articles in a browser.
  • Check for broken links with your usual tool.

Then measure. Watch indexing and clicks in Google Search Console over the following weeks. Results from one site do not guarantee results on yours.

9. Using the same loop for other SEO work

The audit, report, plan, implement and re-audit loop works for other SEO tasks too:

  • Missing or duplicate titles and meta descriptions
  • Schema markup
  • hreflang and language tags
  • Image alt text

FAQ

Can Codex do SEO?

It can audit and edit a site's code and content in bulk. You still choose the strategy and review the results.

How many internal links should a page have?

There is no official number. The forum post I used suggested 10 or more. Treat it as a test, not a rule.

Will Codex break my site?

It can make mistakes. Work on a branch, review the diff and run the project’s relevant checks before deploying.

Which model should I use?

For the audit and the plan I used the lightest model I had, partly because I was low on credits. For the implementation, which ran for about 21 minutes, I switched to a stronger model but skipped the largest one as overkill. Test candidates on a small change and keep the cheapest one that passes your checks.

Does this work with Claude Code?

Yes. The loop is just research, prompts and files in your repo, so it carries over. I mention both tools in the video but only ran it in Codex. Put your link rules in the project instruction file your agent reads, which for Codex is AGENTS.md.

Independent check against Google’s guidance

Google Search Central’s link guidance, read November 7, 2026 ICT, recommends crawlable links and descriptive, relevant anchor text. It explicitly says there is no magical ideal number of links on a page. This overrides any reading of the ten-link anecdote in my video as an official minimum. Keep user relevance and valid destinations ahead of a numeric quota.
