Runable AI Audit-Service Website: Research to an MVP

Quick answer: Write a master brief first, let Runable research the market, then feed both into the build. I got a polished first version of an audit-service website in about 20 minutes and a cleaner homepage after one focused revision. Stripe payments and the consultation email were still not connected when I finished, so a complete-looking site is not yet a business that can take orders.

In this course I used Runable to take one business idea, an AI Growth Audit service, from a brief to a working first version. The offer helps businesses see what may be limiting their search and AI visibility and turns that into a prioritized action plan. I went from the brief to research, a first website and a revised homepage.

The useful part is seeing where it stopped. A first website can look finished while the payment and delivery systems behind it are still missing, and I'll show you exactly which pieces those were.

Write the business brief before the page prompt

I wrote the master brief in ChatGPT before opening Runable. It covers six things: the audience, the problem, the offer, the key promise, the conversion goal and the design direction. I wanted the site to feel like a specialist advisory service, with clear typography, restrained color, strong spacing, simple calls to action and proof-oriented sections.

The brief ends with constraints. No exaggerated claims, no fake testimonials and no invented statistics. If the system needs a fact I haven't supplied, it should use a clear placeholder instead of making one up.

The reason is consistency. When you build through conversation it is easy to drift. One prompt asks for something premium, the next for something playful, another adds a service you never offered, and after ten revisions the site no longer feels like one business. The brief is the source of truth you check every new idea against.

Research the offer, then inspect the evidence

Next I pasted a detailed market-research prompt into Runable. It looked at direct competitors, adjacent services, SEO audit companies, AI visibility platforms and conversion consultants. It also looked at what customers complain about, which objections come up and which segments fit the offer best.

It took about 25 minutes and 19 steps and delivered seven files, including a 36-section research plan, a competitor database, a pricing comparison and an SEO opportunity map. Its recommendation was to test a $595 to $795 core audit before building software or recurring monitoring. The research used about 2,300 credits.

Treat that research as a starting point. The competitor prices and the suggested price range came from an agent's research for my example, not from me checking each company. Open the cited pages yourself before you use any number in your own offer.

Keep a template detour from replacing the brief

When I asked Runable to turn the research into a website, it recommended an interactive research site. I chose the customer-facing marketing site instead. It then offered editorial templates to pick from. I previewed a few, then skipped them, because I wanted the build to follow my master brief.

Templates aren't wrong. The point is that a template should support the offer, not quietly redefine it. When you combine research, attachments and design references, say which one wins. I told Runable to use all of the research above together with the attached master prompt.

Generate a first version and read its launch checklist

About 20 minutes later I had a website with a customer portal, plus an owner configuration and launch checklist as a markdown file. I liked the design straight away. It had a chatbot, a “Get your audit” call to action, a consultation booking path, an FAQ section I'd asked for and a contact form. Google sign-in worked when I tried it in the preview.

The launch checklist was the most honest part. It listed the environment variables and said payments were not implemented, so Stripe still needed adding. There was no pricing page yet. I also suspected the contact form wasn't actually sending email.

Read both the visible site and that checklist. Test where a form actually sends before you trust a “Send inquiry” button. A chat panel also doesn't prove the paid service can be delivered.

Refine one commercial decision at a time

Before prompting a fix, I reviewed the page against five questions: is the hero easy to scan, is the visual hierarchy clear, does the page build trust without invented testimonials, is the copy repeating itself, and is anything there just for decoration? Then I gave Runable one focused prompt:

Refine the homepage without changing the core offer.
1. Make the hero simpler and more credible. The headline should emphasize identifying what is limiting search and AI visibility.
2. Make Get your audit the strongest visual CTA.
3. Reduce repetitive copy across sections.
4. Improve spacing and visual hierarchy so the page is easier to scan.
5. Strengthen trust using process transparency.
6. Remove decorative elements that do not help comprehension or conversion.
Do not add invented proof or claims.

The new headline read “Identify what's limiting your search and AI visibility.” “Get your audit” became the dominant button, with the consultation secondary. Decorative charts and floating report graphics were gone, replaced by a simple sample report preview and clearer stages for scope, customer input, review and handoff. It was simpler, and I thought better.

The pattern is reusable: build a draft, inspect it, fix the biggest weakness, repeat. “Make it better” gives the agent too much room to change the offer.

A scoping checklist before you start

Before you build your own version, settle these. They come from what tripped me up, plus Runable's current pricing page and FAQ:

  • The offer in one sentence. What the customer receives, and what action the page should ask for.
  • A credit budget. The free plan lists 1,500 credits a day, and the FAQ says unused daily credits don't carry over. Research alone used about 2,300 credits for me, so plan for a paid plan or a top-up. The FAQ says detailed usage is in Settings under Token Usage.
  • Payments. The pricing page lists Stripe integration among the paid plans' website features. Decide which plan you need before you promise checkout.
  • Where inquiries go. Name the email address or tool a form should deliver to, then test it.
  • What stays a placeholder. Testimonials, statistics and prices you haven't verified.

Define what is still unfinished

When I finished, the homepage was in good shape, but Stripe wasn't connected and the consultation form wasn't sending to an email address. Both were left for later modules. Before a real launch, payments, inquiry delivery, access control and the actual audit work each need their own check. I didn't take a payment or test with a real customer.

The official Runable site pitches one agent to build, run and grow a business. That is the direction, but what I built here was a strong first version, not a site ready to accept orders.

FAQ

Did Runable build a whole working business?

No. In about 20 minutes it built a polished first version with a chatbot, Google sign-in, an FAQ and a contact form, and one prompt improved the homepage. Its own launch checklist said payments weren't implemented, and the consultation form wasn't sending email yet. I left Stripe and email for later modules.

Should I copy the audit prices from the video?

No. They came from Runable's research for my example business, and I didn't verify each competitor's price. Check the original pages and work out your own costs, market and deliverables first.

How many credits did it use?

The market research used about 2,300 credits. When I recorded, top-ups started at $10 for 10,000 credits. Runable's current pricing page lists 1,500 free credits a day, and its FAQ says you can see detailed usage in Settings under Token Usage.
